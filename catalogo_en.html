Guide by subject · Illustrious Piarists in the classroom

Material for secondary-school teachers: 46 activities of 10-15 minutes, organised into 11 subject groups and built from the profiles in this catalogue. Each activity indicates the subject and knowledge areas of the curriculum (Royal Decree 217/2022, minimum content of Spanish ESO), the key competences it develops, a question for discussion and a link to the character's full profile.

How to use it

  • Choose the group for your subject and a character: the profile has everything you need to prepare the class.
  • Each activity fits in 10-15 minutes and needs no special material.
  • The "Copy subject group" button takes the text to your lesson plan or a document.
  • They are proposals: adapt them to your year, your group and your school.

Key competences (Exit profile)

  • CCL Competence: linguistic communication
  • CP Competence: plurilingual
  • STEM Competence: mathematical, science, technology and engineering
  • CD Competence: digital
  • CPSAA Competence: personal, social and learning to learn
  • CC Competence: citizenship
  • CE Competence: entrepreneurial
  • CCEC Competence: cultural awareness and expression

The profiles are also in the app PíasConnect, with portraits and translations.

Language and Literature

ESO curriculum: Spanish Language and Literature
Knowledge areas: Communication · Literary education · Reflection on language · Languages and their speakers

Tales, humour, journalism and poetry written by former Piarist pupils: from the father of Pinocchio to a poet who published his first book at 18.

Carlo Collodi See profile ↓

He wrote "Pinocchio" in instalments and killed him off in chapter 15; readers protested and he had to bring him back.

Topic Literary education · Duration 12 min

Activity Read aloud chapter XVII (the Fairy and "lies with short legs and lies with long noses"). Then, in pairs, write in 8 lines the "to be continued": Collodi had ended the first version with Pinocchio hanged from the Great Oak. How would you save him?

To discuss Why did the readers of the "Giornale per i bambini" protest when Pinocchio died? What do we expect from a story?

CCLCCEC

Antonio Mingote See profile ↓

More than 60 years of daily cartoons in ABC and 600 drawings to illustrate "Don Quixote".

Topic Communication · Duration 12 min

Activity Each pupil draws a one-picture, one-sentence humorous cartoon about life in class, without hurting anyone. Stick them on the wall and the others guess "the joke". You can compare with a Mingote cartoon about Don Quixote.

To discuss What can you say with a drawing that is harder to say in a text?

CCLCCEC

Adolf Ágai See profile ↓

The "youngest revolutionary" of 1848: at 12, he did not have a single coin to cross the bridge from Pest to Buda.

Topic Communication · Duration 12 min

Activity Write a 10-line newspaper report from Ágai's point of view on 15 March 1848: a 12-year-old who sees the revolution but cannot cross the bridge. Headline, lead and a quotation. Later he founded a satirical magazine, "Borsszem Jankó" (1868).

To discuss What does a child see of a revolution that an adult does not? Is a report the same as an opinion piece?

CCLCC

Pere Gimferrer See profile ↓

He published his first book of poems at 18 and writes in Spanish and Catalan.

Topic Languages and their speakers · Duration 12 min

Activity Choose an important word (home, sea, friend…) and write it in all the languages the class knows. In groups, discuss what shade of meaning it has in each and what changes if you write a poem in one language or another. Show the title "Mensaje del tetrarca" (1963): what does it suggest to you?

To discuss Does what you say change depending on the language you say it in?

History

ESO curriculum: Geography and History (History)
Knowledge areas: Societies and territories · Civic commitment · Challenges of today's world

Heroes of three countries, spies with a double life, the pilot of Ferrari's little horse and the first president of a Spanish republic.

Tadeusz Kościuszko See profile ↓

National hero of Poland, Belarus and the United States; he offered to sit an exam in front of Benjamin Franklin.

Topic Societies and territories · Duration 15 min

Activity Divide the class into three teams (Poland, United States and Belarus). Each team prepares a poster in 5 minutes on "why Kościuszko is our hero" and defends it. Key fact: he arrived in America without letters of recommendation, and as an engineer he fortified West Point.

To discuss What makes a person a hero of three countries at once?

CCCCL

Ignác Martinovics See profile ↓

A friar, physicist, spy for the Crown and leader of a revolutionary conspiracy against it.

Topic Civic commitment · Duration 12 min

Activity In a two-column table, note what Martinovics did "for the Crown" (spy for Leopold II) and what he did "against the Crown" (Jacobin secret societies, 1794). It is a story with a tragic ending (executed in 1795): handle it with tact and without morbidity.

To discuss Can you be loyal and rebellious at the same time? What drives someone to live a double life?

CCCPSAA

Francesco Baracca See profile ↓

The Italian ace of the First World War whose black horse ended up as the emblem of Ferrari.

Topic Challenges of today's world · Duration 12 min

Activity Locate the Italian front (Montello) on a map of Europe and compare aviation in 1916 with today's: three differences. Then look for the origin of the "cavallino rampante": Baracca painted it on his plane; Enzo Ferrari adopted it and said the pilot's mother suggested it.

To discuss Why do some soldiers become symbols? And why did a war horse end up on a car?

CCCCEC

Estanislau Figueras See profile ↓

First president of the First Republic: he lasted four months, and with his three successors there were four presidents in one year.

Topic Societies and territories · Duration 15 min

Activity Timeline of 1873: four groups, one president each (Figueras, Pi i Margall, Salmerón, Castelar). Each group explains in 1 minute "why my president fell". Piarist fact: Figueras and Pi i Margall both attended Piarist classrooms in Barcelona, and Pi was Figueras's minister.

To discuss Why did the First Republic last so little?

Geography

ESO curriculum: Geography and History (Geography)
Knowledge areas: Societies and territories · Challenges of today's world · Local and global civic commitment

A cartographer, a geologist who crossed the ocean, a navigator from Mataró who ended up in Buenos Aires and a doctor who survived Hiroshima.

Francisco Coello de Portugal See profile ↓

He drew the great Atlas of Spain of the 19th century: province by province, at a scale of 1:200,000.

Topic Societies and territories · Duration 12 min

Activity Scale in action: with a ruler, measure 5 cm on a map. At 1:200,000 that is 10 real km (5 × 200,000 = 1,000,000 cm). Calculate the real distance between your city and another using the scale of a physical map. You can compare with a digital map screenshot.

To discuss What is a good map for, for a country? Which maps do we use today that Coello could not have imagined?

STEMCD

Ignacy Domeyko See profile ↓

From Vilnius to Chile: a Polish geologist with a mountain range and a mineral named after him.

Topic Societies and territories · Duration 12 min

Activity Trace his life route on a world map: Vilnius → exile in France after the 1830 Uprising → Chile (1838). Mark the Domeyko Range and find out what domeykite is. Debate: what leads a scientist to live on another continent?

To discuss What does someone lose and gain when they have to leave their country? When does a place become "your country"?

CCSTEM

Domingo Matheu See profile ↓

Born in Mataró, a ship's pilot who ended up presiding over Buenos Aires's Junta Grande in 1811.

Topic Societies and territories · Duration 12 min

Activity Draw Matheu's journey from Mataró to Buenos Aires (1793) on the map and compare the sailing voyage with a flight today. Then look for another famous man from Mataró on our list: Miquel Biada, who also crossed the Atlantic, to Venezuela and Cuba.

To discuss Why did so many Catalans and Spaniards emigrate to America? What did they take and what did they leave?

CCCCL

Pedro Arrupe See profile ↓

A doctor from Bilbao, survivor of the Hiroshima bomb and Superior General of the Jesuits.

Topic Challenges of today's world · Duration 12 min

Activity Locate Bilbao, Madrid, Japan and Hiroshima on the map and trace his life: he studies Medicine, joins the Jesuits, goes to Japan and survives the atomic bomb (1945). With tact, talk about how a conflict changes a territory and people. He spent more than a month in a Japanese jail, wrongly accused of espionage.

To discuss What consequences does a war have for territory and people, beyond what is seen in the news?

Mathematics

ESO curriculum: Mathematics
Knowledge areas: Number sense · Spatial sense · Algebraic sense · Stochastic sense

A mathematician who proved that cubes always touch face to face, a friar who counted 10,000 peas, a friend of Calasanz who measured falling bodies and a chess champion from Getafe.

György Hajós See profile ↓

At 17 he won first prize in a national school mathematics competition. In 1941 he solved Minkowski's conjecture.

Topic Spatial sense · Duration 15 min

Activity Cover a squared sheet with equal squares placed in shifted rows, trying to ensure that no pair of squares shares a full side. Check that it is impossible: it is the plane version of what Hajós proved, with group theory, for cubes in any dimension. Then colour a map with only four colours: the problem he devoted half his life to.

To discuss Why is it worth proving something that seems obvious?

STEMCPSAA

Gregor Mendel See profile ↓

"The pea friar": more than 10,000 plants and laws that nobody read for 30 years.

Topic Stochastic sense · Duration 12 min

Activity Simulate Mendel's cross with two coins (heads = dominant allele, tails = recessive): 40 tosses per pair and a count of results. Do you get 3:1? And with 400 tosses across the whole class? Mendel presented his laws in 1865; they were rediscovered in 1900.

To discuss How many times must an experiment be repeated before you can trust the result?

STEMCPSAA

Galileo Galilei See profile ↓

A friend of Calasanz: the founder of the Pious Schools sent his best Piarists to study with him.

Topic Algebraic sense · Duration 15 min

Activity Let a ball roll down a ramp (a book and a ruler) and measure how long it takes to travel 1, 2 and 3 hand-spans. Check that the distance grows with the square of the time. Tell the context: after the 1633 trial, Calasanz ordered that Galileo be helped, and in 1637 the Piarist Clemente Settimi was his secretary when he went blind.

To discuss Why was it dangerous to be Galileo's friend? What did Calasanz risk?

STEMCCL

Jaime Rey Martínez See profile ↓

Chess player from Getafe, Spanish Under-16 champion, with an Elo of 2342 at 13.

Topic Number sense · Duration 10 min

Activity The chessboard problem: put one grain of wheat on the first square, two on the second, four on the third… How many grains are on square 64? Use a calculator to see how a power of 2 grows. Then ask why chess trains the mind. Jaime learned at 4 and says: "chess makes me happy".

To discuss What does it take to become a champion: talent, training or both?

Science (Biology, Geology, Physics and Chemistry)

ESO curriculum: Biology and Geology · Physics and Chemistry
Knowledge areas: Scientific project · Health and disease · Human body · Matter · Change

A surgeon who revolutionised care for the wounded, another who transplants hands and faces, a chemist who hid two Nobel medals in acid and a physicist who got the lowest mark.

George de Hevesy See profile ↓

Nobel Prize in Chemistry: he dissolved two Nobel medals in acid to hide them from the Nazis and recovered them afterwards.

Topic Matter · Change · Duration 15 min

Activity Drop a spot of colouring into a glass of water and watch how it spreads: it is the idea of the "tracer", the method Hevesy invented in 1913 to follow an atom inside a body. Then discuss how a medal could be "hidden" and recovered (gold dissolves in aqua regia).

To discuss What can chemistry do to protect something valuable in difficult times?

STEMCPSAA

Pedro Cavadas See profile ↓

The "Doctor Miracle": Spain's first face transplant and more than 11,000 free operations in Africa.

Topic Human body · Health and disease · Duration 15 min

Activity Body map: draw the silhouette of a hand and mark skin, bone, muscle, nerve and blood vessel. Cavadas performed Spain's first double hand and forearm transplant in 2006. Then talk about organ donation: what do we need to know and what do we think we know?

To discuss What changes a person's life more: an operation or someone looking after them for free?

STEMCC

Josep Trueta See profile ↓

His method for treating war wounds saved thousands of lives and limbs.

Topic Health and disease · Duration 12 min

Activity Put in order the steps of the Trueta method (clean the wound thoroughly, remove all dead tissue, do not close it, immobilise with plaster and leave it for weeks) and explain in your own words why infections fall this way. Then compare with how a wound is treated at home.

To discuss What does it take for a simple idea to save thousands of people?

STEMCC

Loránd Eötvös See profile ↓

He got the lowest mark in Physics and ended up naming a principle that Einstein used for relativity.

Topic Interaction · Duration 10 min

Activity Drop a rubber ball and a tennis ball (or a big book and a crumpled sheet) at the same time from the same height. Which lands first? It is the intuition of the equivalence between gravitational and inertial mass, which Eötvös measured with his torsion balance, still used in geophysics.

To discuss What counts more: the mark on an exam or what you do afterwards with what you know?

Physical Education

ESO curriculum: Physical Education
Knowledge areas: Active and healthy life · Expressions of motor culture · Emotional self-regulation and social interaction in motor situations

The first great idol of Spanish football, one of the five of the Quinta del Buitre, a world-champion skater and an athlete who is also a doctor of Medicine.

Rafael Moreno «Pichichi» See profile ↓

83 goals in 89 matches and a white handkerchief on his head; he gives his name to the top scorer's trophy.

Topic Expressions of motor culture · Duration 15 min

Activity Team calculation: Pichichi scored 83 goals in 89 official matches: how many goals per match is that? Then a 10-minute session with passing and finishing games. Tell that he was the first to score in San Mamés (1913) and that his bust receives flowers from visiting teams.

To discuss What team values does the top scorer defend?

Emilio Butragueño See profile ↓

"El Buitre", one of the five canteranos of the Quinta del Buitre: he was educated in a Piarist school.

FRANCISCO COELLO DE PORTUGAL Y QUESADA

Military engineer and cartographer

Express Biography

Francisco Coello de Portugal y Quesada (Jaén, 1822 – Madrid, 1898), military engineer and cartographer. He drew the “Atlas of Spain and its Overseas Possessions”, the great Spanish cartographic work of the 19th century, and was one of the founders of the Geographical Society of Madrid.

At the Pious Schools

Born in Jaén on 26 April 1822. According to the biography of the Royal Academy of History, he received his primary education with the Piarists of Getafe. In 1836 he entered the Military Engineers Academy in Guadalajara, where he studied until 1839 and graduated as a lieutenant.

Career

  • In 1840 he took part in the last campaigns of the First Carlist War, in the Maestrazgo and on Espartero's side, and in 1841 he received the Cross of San Fernando.
  • Around 1844 he was military attaché to the French army in Algeria for about two years; there his interest in cartography was awakened.
  • He drew the maps for Pascual Madoz's “Geographical-Statistical-Historical Dictionary” and then published them separately as the “Atlas of Spain and its Overseas Possessions”.
  • The provincial sheets, at 1:200,000, came out one by one between 1847 and 1870. The project planned 65 sheets, but eleven provinces were left undrawn.
  • In 1855 he published the “Project for the general lines of navigation and railways in the Iberian Peninsula”, with a radial and transversal network.
  • From 1858 he worked in the Statistics Commission, helped draft the Land Measurement Law (1859) and headed the topographic-cadastral section of the General Board of Statistics (1861) until his forced resignation in 1866.
  • He joined the Royal Academy of History on 27 December 1874.
  • In March 1876 he was one of the founders of the Geographical Society of Madrid, of which he was honorary president from 1878 until his death.
  • In 1881 he proposed the creation of the Spanish Society of Africanists and Colonists, founded in 1883 with Joaquín Costa.
  • He died in Madrid on 30 September 1898 and was buried in the San Justo cemetery.

Did you know?

  • He left his Atlas unfinished: the State withdrew funding when the first sheet of the National Topographic Map appeared in 1875.
  • He married Aurora Pacheco Casani on 19 October 1849 and they had four children.
  • Money problems led him to lose, on 1 January 1869, his spa “Los Hervideros de Fuensanta”, near Pozuelo de Calatrava.
  • He was an Iberist: he believed that better communications would bring Spain and Portugal closer.
  • His brother Diego (1820-1897) was a lawyer, diplomat and politician.

Legacy

  • His “Atlas of Spain” remains a reference for knowing the Spain of the mid-19th century.
  • He was a pioneer of modern geography in Spain and a promoter of its study through the Geographical Society of Madrid.
  • His railway network project anticipated the radial structure of Spanish communications.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Science useful to all: a map teaches a country to know itself, the same mission Calasanz gave to the school.

  • 1847-1870 Publishes the sheets of his “Atlas of Spain”

SEGISMUNDO MORET

Liberal politician, economist and orator; prime minister

Express Biography

Segismundo Moret y Prendergast (Cádiz, 1833 – Madrid, 1913), liberal politician, economist and great orator. He was a minister many times and prime minister three times between 1905 and 1910. In 1870 he drove the law that took the first steps against slavery in the Spanish colonies.

At the Pious Schools

Born in Cádiz, from a family of Irish descent on his mother's side (the Prendergasts). The date of his birth varies by source: the most usual is 2 June 1833, but 1836 and 1838 are also cited. According to the biography of the Royal Academy of History, he studied with the Piarists of Getafe before 1853. He read law at the Central University of Madrid, and in 1858, while still very young, he won there the chair of Public Finance.

Career

  • In 1863 he was elected independent deputy for Almadén and in 1868 he returned to the Cortes for Ciudad Real after the Revolution; he helped draft the Constitution of 1869.
  • As Overseas Minister in 1870 he promoted the Moret Law (4 July 1870) of “free birth”: the children of enslaved women would be born free and slaves over sixty were freed.
  • He was Finance Minister between 1870 and 1871.
  • In 1875 he founded the Democratic-Monarchist Party, which ended up absorbed into Sagasta's Liberal Party.
  • He was Interior Minister in 1883-1884, 1888 and 1901-1902, and Minister of State (Foreign Affairs) in 1885-1888 and again in the 1890s.
  • He presided over the Royal Geographical Society between 1885 and 1887 and the Ateneo of Madrid in several periods, the last from 1899 until his death.
  • In 1897-1898, again Overseas Minister, he decreed autonomy for Cuba and Puerto Rico, and opposed the war with the United States.
  • He headed the government from 1 December 1905 to 6 July 1906; from 30 November to 4 December 1906 (barely 48 hours); and from 21 October 1909 to 9 February 1910, after the Tragic Week.
  • He presided over the Congress of Deputies in 1901-1902 and again from 1912 until his death on 28 January 1913.

Did you know?

  • His government of November 1906 is one of the shortest in Spanish history: it lasted about 48 hours.
  • In 1894 he was elected to the Royal Spanish Academy, although he never took his seat.
  • He was named adopted son of Cáceres (1881) and favourite son of Cádiz (1907).
  • He headed the Ateneo of Madrid for much of his last thirty years, and his oratory was famous.

Legacy

  • The Moret Law of 1870 opened the way to the abolition of slavery in the Spanish colonies.
  • His decree of autonomy for Cuba and Puerto Rico (1897) was one of the last attempts to avoid the break with the colonies.
  • He is one of the great figures of Spanish liberalism of the Restoration.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Defending the freedom of the most defenceless: his 1870 law thought above all of the children of slaves, an echo of Calasanz's conviction that every child deserves a chance.

  • 1870 Moret Law (4 July)
  • 1897 Decrees autonomy for Cuba and Puerto Rico
  • 1905 First premiership (1 December)
  • 1909 Third premiership (21 October)

JOSEP TRUETA

Orthopaedic surgeon, creator of the “Trueta method”

Express Biography

Josep Trueta i Raspall (Barcelona, 1897 – 1977), Catalan surgeon and orthopaedist. In the Civil War he developed a method of treating open wounds and fractures that saved thousands of lives and limbs, and that was later applied in the Second World War. He ended up as a professor at the University of Oxford.

At the Pious Schools

Born in Barcelona on 27 October 1897. According to WikiPía he studied with the Piarists of Barcelona (San Antón, in Calle Manso). He took his medical degree at the University of Barcelona around 1921 and trained as a surgeon at the Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau, alongside his master, the surgeon Manuel Corachan.

Career

  • In the Civil War he directed the care of the wounded at the Hospital de Sant Pau in Barcelona and treated thousands of casualties, among them the victims of the bombing of the city in 1938.
  • His method consisted of thoroughly cleaning the wound, removing all dead tissue, not closing it and leaving the limb immobilised in plaster for weeks: infections, amputations and mortality fell dramatically.
  • In 1939 he went into exile in England, and that same year he published, in English, “Treatment of War Wounds and Fractures”.
  • In Oxford he worked at the Radcliffe Infirmary and organised an accident service, and in 1943 he published “The Principles and Practice of War Surgery”.
  • During the Second World War his method was applied in British hospitals, with excellent results among the wounded.
  • In 1946 he published, in English, “The Spirit of Catalonia”, an essay on the history and identity of his country.
  • In 1949 he was appointed Nuffield Professor of Orthopaedic Surgery at Oxford, a post he held until 1966.
  • That year he returned to Barcelona, where he died on 29 January 1977.

Did you know?

  • His method was based on the closed-plaster treatment already popularised by the American surgeon H. Winnett Orr; Trueta always acknowledged that debt and perfected it in the operating theatres of Barcelona.
  • He learned to operate in a city at war: many of his assistants were students and young doctors.
  • The University Hospital of Girona now bears his name: “Doctor Josep Trueta”.
  • Although he became famous in the English-speaking world, he never stopped regarding himself as Catalan and defended the language and culture of his country.

Legacy

  • His method of treating open wounds and fractures became doctrine in war and accident surgery.
  • He is considered one of the great Catalan doctors of the 20th century and a key figure of modern orthopaedics.
  • The University Hospital Doctor Josep Trueta in Girona keeps his name alive.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Caring for the weakest: Trueta treated civilians and soldiers with the same dedication, just as Calasanz wanted the school to serve all children.

FRANCESC PI I MARGALL

Politician, philosopher and writer; president of the First Republic

Express Biography

Francesc Pi i Margall (Barcelona, 1824 – Madrid, 1901), politician, philosopher, historian and writer, theorist of federalism. He was president of the Executive Power of the First Spanish Republic in 1873 and is one of the great figures of republicanism and of 19th-century Spanish political thought.

At the Pious Schools

Born in Barcelona on 29 April 1824 (some sources give 20 April), the son of a textile manufacturer. According to the biography of the Royal Academy of History, he studied with the Piarists of Barcelona from the age of seven; the 1991 yearbook specifies that it was at San Antón. At 17 he entered the University of Barcelona, where he studied Philosophy and then Law, and in 1847 he moved to Madrid, where he took his doctorate in Law at 24.

Career

  • He first devoted himself to literature and art criticism: in 1851 he published a “History of Painting”, which the Church banned.
  • In 1848 he joined the Democratic Party.
  • In 1854-1855 he published “La reacción y la revolución”, his great doctrinal statement, and became the main translator and spreader of Proudhon's thought in Spain.
  • He lost by very few votes the 1854 Cortes elections for Barcelona against General Prim.
  • After the Revolution of 1868 he was elected deputy for Barcelona, although he had not campaigned, and in 1870 he took over the leadership of the Federal Democratic Republican Party.
  • Between 12 February and 18 July 1873 he was Interior Minister under Estanislao Figueras.
  • From 11 June to 18 July 1873 he presided over the Executive Power of the First Republic; he resigned over the Cantonal Rebellion of Cartagena, after failed negotiations with the cantonalists.
  • After Pavía's coup he published “La República de 1873” (1874), a work that was banned.
  • He continued with “Las nacionalidades” (1877), “La Federación” (1880) and the “Programme of the Federal Party” (1894).
  • He died in Madrid on 29 November 1901; some 12,000 people attended his funeral at the civil cemetery.

Did you know?

  • He defended a federal republic built “from the bottom up”, through pacts between municipalities and regions.
  • He rejected insurrection and bet on the legal route: he held that political change had to be won by persuasion.
  • He presided over the First Republic for barely 37 days, from 11 June to 18 July 1873.
  • His birth date appears as 29 April in most biographies, although some give 20 April.

Legacy

  • He is the great theorist of Spanish federalism and a reference point of republicanism.
  • His work “Las nacionalidades” is still cited in the debate on the territorial organisation of Spain.
  • His thought influenced the Spanish labour and libertarian movement, according to the scholar Rudolf Rocker.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Educating for freedom and equality: his federalism trusted in an educated people, a conviction very close to Calasanz's popular school.

ESTANISLAU FIGUERAS

Lawyer and politician; first president of the First Republic

Express Biography

Estanislau Figueras i Moragas (Barcelona, 1819 – Madrid, 1882), lawyer and federal republican politician. He was the first president of the Executive Power of the First Spanish Republic, between 11 February and 11 June 1873.

At the Pious Schools

Born in Barcelona on 13 November 1819. According to the sketch “Socios ilustres”, he studied for five years as a boarder with the Piarists of Barcelona; the 1991 yearbook specifies that it was at San Antón. He took his law degree in 1844 and moved to Tarragona to practise as a lawyer.

Career

  • In 1849 he joined the Democratic Party and in 1851 he was elected deputy for Tarragona.
  • He presided over the Revolutionary Junta of Tarragona during the events that led to the Progressive Biennium (1854-1856).
  • In 1855 he was again a deputy and led the republican minority in the Cortes; he voted in favour of a republican regime, which the Chamber rejected.
  • In 1856, in the constitutional debate, he defended decentralising the State.
  • In 1867, after the failure of the San Gil barracks uprising, he was sentenced to prison and fled to Portugal.
  • After the September Revolution of 1868 he returned, joined Pi i Margall's Federal Party and founded the newspaper “La Igualdad”.
  • After Amadeo I's abdication, the National Assembly proclaimed the Republic on 11 February 1873 and elected him first president of the Executive Power.
  • On 9 March 1873 the Estat Català was proclaimed in Barcelona within the federal Republic; Figueras managed to reverse it.
  • On 22 March 1873 the Cortes abolished slavery in Puerto Rico.
  • In the May 1873 elections the federals won an overwhelming majority.
  • On 11 June 1873 he left the presidency in the hands of Pi i Margall and fled to France.
  • With the Restoration he led the “organic” wing of federalism, at odds with Pi i Margall.
  • He died in Madrid on 11 November 1882 and was buried in the Civil Cemetery.

Did you know?

  • From his last Council of Ministers he is credited with a very colloquial phrase saying that he could stand no more: it is one of the most famous of the First Republic.
  • The First Republic had four presidents in less than a year: Figueras, Pi i Margall, Salmerón and Castelar.
  • Two of them, Figueras and Pi i Margall, passed through the Piarist classrooms of Barcelona; Pi i Margall was his Interior Minister.
  • He left power after barely four months, amid an economic crisis, the Carlist war and divisions within his own party.

Legacy

  • He was the first president of a republic in the history of Spain.
  • Under his government the Cortes abolished slavery in Puerto Rico.
  • His figure sums up the hopes and difficulties of 19th-century Spanish federal republicanism.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Public service and firm convictions: an education that taught to think and to serve accompanied the man who wanted a State closer to the people.

  • 1844 Takes his law degree

  • 1868 Returns after the Revolution and founds “La Igualdad”
  • 1873 First president of the Executive Power of the Republic (11 February-11 June)

SAN JOSÉ DE CALASANZ

Founder of the Order of the Pious Schools — the central figure of the whole project

Express Biography

Aragonese priest born in Peralta de la Sal (Huesca) on 31 July 1557, the seventh and last child of a family of infanzones (minor Aragonese nobility; his father had a forge and became mayor of the village). Founder of the Pious Schools, the first religious order in history devoted entirely to free education for ordinary people, and today Universal Patron of all Christian popular schools in the world. He is, quite simply, the origin of everything else: without him, none of the other figures in this catalogue would exist.

Did you know?

  • His motto, “Piety and Letters” (today we might say “faith and culture”), sums up in two words the heart of his whole educational project: forming complete people, not just instructed ones.
  • He left very few systematic texts on his educational thinking, but more than 10,000 letters, which today are the main source for understanding his idea of education.
  • He was a contemporary of the great Czech educator Comenius (Jan Amos Komenský), though from different religious traditions (one Catholic, the other a Protestant bishop); each, in his own way, defended education for all.
  • Spain celebrates Teachers' Day on 27 November in his honour.
  • Over time, his charism gave rise to several sister congregations still active today: the Escolapias (founded by Paula Montal), the Calasanctian Sisters (founded by Saint Faustino Míguez, linked to Getafe), and other orders in various countries.

Legacy

  • He was the first man in the history of Europe to create a free education system open to any child, whatever their social class. That model —a popular, free and high-quality school— is the direct seed of modern public education as we understand it today. More than four centuries later, his Order is still active in dozens of countries on five continents, and every single figure in this catalogue —from Cajal to Amenábar, from Gaudí to Karol G— is ultimately the fruit of the seed he planted in a small borrowed room in Rome in 1597.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Piety and letters. Faith and culture. The conviction, centuries ahead of its time, that an integral education —spiritual and intellectual— is the most powerful instrument of personal and social transformation there is. And, above all, a perseverance that withstood failure, envy, intrigue and even his own removal from office: he never stopped believing in the project, not even when it was on the verge of disappearing.

  • A lunar crater and an asteroid (117413 Ramonycajal) bear his name.

Legacy

  • He is regarded as the father of modern neuroscience: his neuron doctrine is still the basis for understanding the brain. His legacy of drawings is kept in Madrid.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Tenacity, curiosity and work discipline: he argued that willpower can be trained.

  • Childhood Primary school with the Piarists of Jaca; secondary school in Huesca
  • 1877 Doctorate in Medicine

  • 1934 Dies in Madrid

OUTSTANDING POLISH PIARIST RELIGIOUS

PADRE ADAM SŁOTWIŃSKI

Piarist, patriotic preacher and conspirator — rebuilder of the Polish Province

Express Biography

Polish Piarist priest (1834 — Kraków, 1894), one of the most unusual figures in this catalogue: a religious, a fiery preacher, an independence conspirator and, later, the rebuilder of the whole Piarist presence in Poland.

At the Pious Schools

A member of the Piarist Order. Decades after his activist years, he became rector of the Piarist College of Kraków (1879-1888), which he devoted much of his life to rebuilding after years of weakness.

Did you know?

  • He wrote his own memoirs, Memories of a Recent Past (1892), in which he did not hesitate to criticise other priests of his time for their indifference to the national cause.
  • His contemporaries described him as “a man of great courage” (magnae fortitudinis vir), although some religious of other orders viewed him with suspicion because of his close links with “patriotic” circles.
  • His life combines, in an unusual way, religious vocation, risky political preaching, armed conspiracy and, decades later, patient institutional rebuilding.

Legacy

  • Thanks to his rebuilding work, the Piarist Order kept a continuous presence in Poland well into the 20th century, in a country that for generations was divided between foreign powers.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Civic commitment taken to the limit: a religious ready to risk prison and exile for the freedom of his country, who then devoted the rest of his life, with the same dedication, to rebuilding the institution that had formed him.

Timeline

  • Before 1863 Exiled to Russia for patriotic demonstrations
  • Advent 1862 Patriotic sermons in Stoczek Łukowski and Chełm
  • 1863 Settles in Kraków; the Piarist college becomes a meeting point for conspirators
  • 1864 Takes de facto command in Kraków; later flees to Paris
  • 1871 Returns to Kraków after the fall of Paris
  • 1879-1888 Rector of the Piarist College of Kraków, which he rebuilds
  • 1892 Publishes his memoirs
  • 1894 Dies in Kraków

PADRE JAKUB FALKOWSKI

Founder of the Institute for the Deaf in Warsaw

Express Biography

Polish Piarist priest (Budlewo, 1775 — Warsaw, 1848), educator and philanthropist, called the “Polish Charles de l'Épée” (after the founder of the first school for the deaf in France).

Career

  • As a teacher in Łomża, Drohiczyn and Szczuczyn, he met a 7-year-old deaf boy, Piotr Gąsowski, who changed the course of his life.
  • 1803-1804: studied deaf education in Leipzig.
  • 1817: founds in Warsaw the Institute for the Deaf, the first school in Poland for deaf children, still running today.

Did you know?

  • The first sentence his pupils learnt was, deliberately, “I am a person” — so that they would understand their own dignity above all.
  • In 1822 the Tsar and King of Poland, Alexander I, visited the institute and donated funds to enlarge the building.
  • The pupils learnt trades (shoemaker, carpenter, tailor, bookbinder) and the most gifted ones, drawing and painting.

Legacy

  • The Warsaw Institute for the Deaf, today the Jakub Falkowski Institute, is still running more than 200 years after it was founded.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Care for the most vulnerable: he took the Calasanzian spirit of “education for all” to a group that in his day had no educational opportunity at all.

Timeline

  • 1775 Born in Budlewo
  • 1800 Ordained priest
  • 1803-1804 Studies deaf education in Leipzig
  • 1817 Founds the Warsaw Institute for the Deaf
  • 1848 Dies in Warsaw

Famous quote

“I am a person.” — The first sentence taught to the institute's deaf pupils, as a declaration of dignity.

PADRE ONUFRY KOPCZYŃSKI

Creator of modern Polish grammar

Express Biography

Polish Piarist priest, grammarian, linguist and Latin poet (Czerniejewo, 1735 — Warsaw, 1817), a key figure of the Polish Enlightenment.

At the Pious Schools

He entered the Piarist Order in 1752, taking the religious name Onufry.

Career

  • Teacher of grammar, poetry and rhetoric in several Piarist schools, and at the prestigious Collegium Nobilium.
  • Member of the Society for Elementary Books.
  • 1785: publishes his Grammar for the National Schools, the founding work of modern Polish grammar.

Did you know?

  • Polish grammatical terms for “noun”, “adjective”, “conjunction” and the punctuation marks were popularised thanks to his work.
  • He also wrote a basic reading and writing primer based on the daily life of country children.
  • He personally received a gold medal for his grammar at the celebration of his 81st birthday, a few months before he died.
  • After the November Uprising, his remains were secretly moved to an unmarked common grave when the Russian authorities seized the church where he lay.

Legacy

  • His grammar is the basis of modern Polish teaching, and it helped preserve Polish national identity at a time when the partitions had wiped the country off the map of Europe.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Defending one's own identity and culture through education, in a context of enormous political adversity.

Timeline

  • 1735 Born in Czerniejewo
  • 1752 Enters the Piarist Order
  • 1785 Publishes his Grammar for the National Schools
  • 1817 Dies in Warsaw

PADRE RAFAŁ CZERWIAKOWSKI

“Father of Polish surgery”

Express Biography

Polish Piarist priest, physician, surgeon and anatomist (near Pinsk, 1743-1816), creator of the first chair of surgery in Poland.

At the Pious Schools

He studied at the Piarist school in Pinsk and entered the Order in 1762, taking his vows in 1765.

Career

  • 1771: sent to Rome to study Medicine, obtaining his doctorate in 1776.
  • Furthered his studies in surgery in Paris and obstetrics in Berlin; practised for three years at the Hospital of the Holy Spirit in Rome.
  • 1779: back in Kraków, he founds the first chair of Surgery and Obstetrics in Poland, at the Kraków Academy (today the Jagiellonian University), at just 36.

Did you know?

  • He invented several surgical instruments of his own, including an extensor for treating bone fractures.
  • He wrote the first surgery manual in Polish.
  • He treated poor students and sick people without means free of charge.

Legacy

  • He is considered the founder of modern surgery, gynaecology and orthopaedics in Poland.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Science at the service of the poorest: he combined medical excellence with free care for those who could not pay.

  • 1762 Enters the Piarist Order
  • 1776 Doctorate in Medicine in Rome

GALILEO GALILEI

Astronomer, physicist and mathematician — a personal friend of Saint Joseph Calasanz

Express Biography

Italian astronomer and physicist (Pisa, 1564 — Arcetri, 1642), father of modern astronomy.

At the Pious Schools

A personal friend of Calasanz. He was not a pupil (his education predates the Order), but he kept up an exceptional scientific collaboration with it.

  • There is a specific academic study of this collaboration (“Galileo among the Piarists”).

Legacy

  • The Order backed modern science even at the risk of reprisals.

The mark of Calasanz

  • Intellectual courage and friendship with the truth, despite the institutional risk.

TOMMASO CAMPANELLA

Philosopher and Dominican friar — lived with the Piarists and wrote in their defence

Express Biography

Italian philosopher, theologian and poet (Stilo, 1568 — Paris, 1639), Dominican friar, author of the utopia The City of the Sun. He spent 27 years in prison, accused of heresy and of conspiring against Spanish rule in Calabria.

At the Pious Schools

He was not a pupil (he was educated in the Dominican Order), but he had an exceptionally close relationship with the Pious Schools in the last stage of his life: between 1629 and 1632, after his release from prison, he lived with the Piarist fathers in Frascati, where he even taught classes himself. In those same years he wrote the Apologia pro Scholis Piis (“Defence of the Pious Schools”), a text defending the Order against attacks from the civil nobility and, above all, from the Society of Jesus.

Career

  • He entered the Dominican Order at 15, the son of an illiterate shoemaker.
  • Tried several times for heresy; imprisoned for 27 years (1599-1626) after being accused of leading a conspiracy against Spanish rule in Calabria.
  • In captivity he wrote his most important works, including The City of the Sun (1602).
  • He publicly defended Galileo at his first trial (1616) and again in 1622.
  • Released in 1626, he spent his last years between Rome, Frascati and finally Paris, under the protection of King Louis XIII.

Did you know?

  • The manuscript of the Apologia pro Scholis Piis was considered lost for three centuries: it was only recovered in 1932, when the Procurator General of the Pious Schools found a copy in the archive of the Piarist college of Carcare (Liguria). It is still the only known copy.
  • In that same period, the historian Ludwig von Pastor described the Pious Schools as “the first free public popular school in Europe”.
  • To escape a new accusation in 1634, he fled Rome disguised as a Minim friar and under a false name.

Legacy

  • His philosophical defence of free education for ordinary people, written from inside the Piarist community of Frascati, is a unique testimony of how one of the great minds of the 17th century saw Calasanz's school as a project of freedom, not just of instruction.

The mark of Calasanz

  • The conviction, shared by Campanella and Calasanz, that educating the people —peasants, craftsmen and sailors, not just nobles— makes society not only more prosperous but freer.

Timeline

  • 1568 Born in Stilo (Calabria)
  • 1583 Enters the Dominican Order, aged 15
  • 1599-1626 27 years in prison
  • 1602 Writes The City of the Sun
  • 1629-1632 Lives with the Piarists in Frascati; writes the Apologia pro Scholis Piis
  • 1634 Flees to France
  • 1639 Dies in Paris

Famous quote

“An educated people does not easily tolerate tyranny.” — Apologetic book against the adversaries of the Institute of the Pious Schools, § 12; critical edition by Maurizio Erto, Fabrizio Serra Editore, 2015 — original Latin: “populus eruditus non facile patitur tyrannidem”. Campanella writes it to argue that popular education, the central mission of the Pious Schools, makes society freer in the face of tyrants, who “feed the ignorance of the people so they can do what they like with them”.

RELIGIOUS OF THE PIARIST ORDER (MEXICO)

FRAY TORMENTA (Sergio Gutiérrez Benítez)

Priest and wrestler, founder of an orphanage

Express Biography

Mexican priest and retired professional wrestler (San Agustín Metzquititlán, Hidalgo, 1945), famous for fighting masked to support the orphanage he founded. His story inspired the film Nacho Libre.

At the Pious Schools

He was not a pupil of a Piarist school: he entered the Order directly as a novice and religious at 22, after overcoming a period of youthful addictions with the help of a Mercedarian religious. He studied philosophy in Navarre (Spain) and then in Italy. He left the Order around 1976, on the advice of a Piarist superior, so that he could stay with the children of his orphanage, becoming a secular priest of the diocese of Texcoco (Mexico). Confirmed by several press sources and by ACI Prensa.

Career

  • He founded in Texcoco (State of Mexico) “La Casa Hogar de los Cachorros de Fray Tormenta”, which came to house up to 270 children.
  • He went into professional wrestling to fund the orphanage, fighting for 23 years under a red and yellow mask.
  • His story inspired four films, including Nacho Libre (2006), with Jack Black.

Did you know?

  • He kept his identity secret for years until another wrestler, Huracán Ramírez, advised him to reveal it.
  • He worked at the orphanage with Father Chinchachoma, also a Piarist.

Legacy

  • More than 2,000 children have passed through the orphanage he founded, many of whom became doctors, engineers, lawyers and civil servants.

The mark of Calasanz

  • The discipline and dedication learnt in the Piarist novitiate, redirected after he left the Order towards a vocation entirely his own: direct care for abandoned children.
